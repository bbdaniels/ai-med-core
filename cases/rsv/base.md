# Evaluation of Respiratory Illness in an Older Adult

## Patient Background

- **Name:** Antanas Jonaitis
- **Age / Gender:** 75-year-old man
- **Occupation / Living Situation:** Retired factory worker (metalwork); lives in a small house near Siauliai with his wife Danute (age 73)
- **Marital / Family Status:** Married; one adult son lives nearby; two grandchildren (ages 6 and 8) who visit often
- **Socioeconomic Context:** State pension; Lithuanian national health insurance (PSDF)
- **Setting:** Outpatient primary care clinic
- **Primary Reason for Visit:** Worsening cough and shortness of breath for the past 5 days
- **Lifestyle / Supports:** Active for his age -- maintains a small garden, walks to the local store; attends church and meets retired friends for coffee; wife is healthy and drives them to appointments

## Chief Complaint & Opening Statement

> "I've had this awful cough for about five days now, and it's getting harder to breathe. My wife says I sound terrible and made me come in."

## History of Present Illness

- **Onset:** Started with nasal congestion and mild sore throat about 7 days ago; progressed to persistent productive cough and increasing dyspnea over the past 5 days
- **Course:** Initially thought it was a common cold; cough has worsened, now producing small amounts of whitish sputum; shortness of breath with minimal exertion (walking to bathroom, getting dressed)
- **Associated Symptoms:** Low-grade fever (measured 37.8C at home), fatigue, decreased appetite, mild wheezing (wife can hear it at night)
- **Aggravating Factors:** Dyspnea worsens with any physical activity; cough worse when lying flat and at night
- **Relieving Factors:** Sitting upright helps breathing; albuterol inhaler provides partial temporary relief
- **Functional Impact:** Unable to tend garden or walk to store for past 3 days; wife has taken over all household tasks; sleeps propped up on pillows
- **Context:** Grandchildren visited 10 days ago; grandson (age 8) had a "bad cold" with runny nose and cough
- **Vaccination Status:** No flu shot this season; no RSV vaccination; COVID booster was over a year ago

## Past Medical History

- COPD (mild-moderate, diagnosed 5 years ago; FEV1 65% predicted at last spirometry)
- Hypertension (well-controlled on lisinopril)
- Benign prostatic hyperplasia
- Remote history of peptic ulcer (resolved)
- Former smoker (30 pack-years, quit 10 years ago)
- No prior hospitalizations for respiratory illness

## Medications

- Tiotropium (Spiriva) 18 mcg inhaler once daily
- Lisinopril 10 mg daily
- Tamsulosin 0.4 mg at bedtime
- Albuterol (salbutamol) inhaler as needed (uses rarely, 1-2x/month usually; increased to 3-4x/day over the past few days)

## Allergies

- Penicillin (hives as a child)

## Family History

- Father: died of lung cancer at age 68 (heavy smoker)
- Mother: died at 82 of stroke; had hypertension and heart failure
- Brother: alive, age 72, has COPD

## Social History

- Lives with wife Danute in a small house near Siauliai
- Retired factory worker (metalwork); possible occupational dust exposure over 35 years
- Former smoker, quit 10 years ago (30 pack-years)
- No alcohol in past 5 years; previously moderate drinker
- Son lives 10 minutes away; grandchildren visit 1-2 times per week
- Active for his age: maintains a small garden, walks to the local store daily
- Well-connected socially: attends church, has a group of retired friends he meets for coffee weekly
- Wife is healthy and supportive; she drives them to appointments and manages household logistics
- No recreational drugs

## Review of Systems (Focused)

- **General:** Fatigue, decreased appetite, low-grade fever, chills; reports feeling "weaker than usual"
- **HEENT:** Nasal congestion improving; mild sore throat (resolved); no ear pain
- **Cardiac:** No chest pain or palpitations; no leg swelling
- **Respiratory:** Productive cough (whitish sputum), dyspnea on minimal exertion, wheezing; denies hemoptysis
- **GI:** Decreased appetite; no nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea
- **Neuro:** No confusion, no headache; alert and oriented
- **MSK:** Mild generalized body aches
- **Psych:** Anxious about breathing difficulty; frustrated about functional decline

## Provider Questions and Scripted SP Responses

> "What brings you in today?"
"This cough started about a week ago -- just a stuffy nose and scratchy throat at first. But over the past five days it's gotten much worse. I'm coughing up some whitish mucus, and I can barely walk to the bathroom without getting winded. My wife Danute says she can hear me wheezing from the next room at night. She made me come in today."

> "How has your breathing been compared to your usual baseline?"
"Much worse. Normally my breathing is fine -- I walk to the store, I work in the garden. Now I can't even get dressed without stopping to catch my breath. I haven't been this bad before."

> "Can you describe the cough in more detail?"
"It's a wet cough -- I'm bringing up some whitish, sticky mucus. Not a lot, but it's there. It's worse at night and when I lie down. I've been sleeping propped up on pillows because of it."

> "Have you had any fever?"
"Yes, my wife checked with the thermometer. It was 37.8 yesterday. I've had chills on and off too. I don't feel feverish right now, but I feel hot and cold in waves."

> "Has anyone around you been sick recently?"
"Yes, our grandson -- he's eight. He was here about ten days ago with a terrible cold, coughing and runny nose. His little sister had it too. We had them for the whole weekend. I figured I caught whatever they had."

> "Tell me about your smoking history."
"I smoked for about twenty-five years, maybe a pack a day. I quit ten years ago after they told me I had COPD. Haven't touched a cigarette since. But I worked in a metal factory for thirty-five years, so I breathed in plenty of dust too."

> "How have you been managing your COPD?"
"I use the Spiriva inhaler every morning, and I have the blue rescue inhaler for when I need it. Usually I only need the blue one once or twice a month. But this past week I've been using it three or four times a day, and it only helps a little."

> "Have you had your flu shot or any other vaccinations recently?"
"No, I didn't get the flu shot this year. I kept meaning to, but I never got around to it. I don't think I've had any vaccine for RSV -- I didn't even know there was one. My last COVID shot was more than a year ago."

> "What medications are you currently taking?"
"The Spiriva inhaler in the morning, my blood pressure pill -- lisinopril -- and the tamsulosin for my prostate at night. And I've been using the albuterol inhaler much more than usual this week."

> "How has this illness affected your daily activities?"
"I haven't been able to do anything for three days. No garden, no walking to the store, nothing. Danute is doing everything. I can barely get to the kitchen for a cup of tea without feeling winded. It's frustrating -- I'm not used to being this helpless."

> "How far can you walk before you need to stop and rest?"
"Right now? Maybe from the bedroom to the bathroom -- that's about ten meters. Then I have to sit down and catch my breath. A week ago I was walking to the store and back, maybe a kilometer each way."

> "Are you eating and drinking normally?"
"Not really. I don't have much appetite. Danute keeps making me soup and tea, and I'm drinking, but I'm not eating like I normally would."

> "What worries you most right now?"
"I don't want to end up in the hospital. My wife would worry too much. Can I just get some medicine and go home? But honestly, the breathing scares me. I've never felt this short of breath before."

## Physical Examination Findings

- **General:** Alert older man, mildly ill-appearing; speaks in full sentences but pauses to catch breath between longer phrases; coughs intermittently during examination
- **Vitals:**
  - BP 142/84 mmHg
  - Heart rate 88 bpm, regular
  - Respiratory rate 22/min
  - SpO2 93% on room air
  - Temperature 37.9C
- **HEENT:** Mild nasal congestion; oropharynx mildly erythematous, no exudates; no sinus tenderness
- **Neck:** No lymphadenopathy; no jugular venous distention
- **Cardiac:** Regular rate and rhythm; no murmurs; no S3 or S4 gallop
- **Respiratory:** Bilateral expiratory wheezes; scattered rhonchi; mild crackles at bilateral bases; prolonged expiratory phase; using accessory muscles slightly; air movement moderately reduced compared to expected
- **Abdomen:** Soft, non-tender, non-distended; normal bowel sounds
- **Extremities:** No peripheral edema; no cyanosis; capillary refill 3 seconds; warm extremities
- **Neurological:** Alert and oriented to person, place, time, and situation; no focal deficits

## Assessment / Differential Diagnosis

1. Acute RSV bronchiolitis/pneumonitis (most likely given sick grandchild contact, progressive URI-to-LRTI pattern, age and risk factors, bilateral wheezes with crackles)
2. COPD exacerbation (possibly triggered by RSV or other viral infection; increased rescue inhaler use, worsening from baseline)
3. Community-acquired pneumonia (bacterial superinfection possible given productive cough, crackles, and fever)
4. Influenza (less likely given gradual onset over 7 days and URI prodrome rather than abrupt onset)
5. COVID-19 (should be ruled out given incomplete vaccination)
6. Heart failure exacerbation (less likely but consider given age, bilateral crackles, dyspnea, and family history of maternal heart failure)

## Management Plan

- **Diagnostic testing:**
  - RSV rapid antigen test and/or PCR
  - COVID-19 rapid test
  - Influenza rapid test
  - Chest X-ray (assess for pneumonia, pleural effusion, heart failure)
  - CBC, CMP, procalcitonin (to help differentiate viral from bacterial etiology)
  - Pulse oximetry monitoring (continuous during visit; home pulse oximeter if available)
- **Respiratory management:**
  - Increase albuterol frequency: nebulizer or MDI with spacer, every 4-6 hours as needed
  - Continue tiotropium daily
  - Consider short course of oral corticosteroids (prednisone 40 mg x 5 days) if significant bronchospasm or COPD exacerbation component
  - Supplemental oxygen if SpO2 remains below 92% on room air
- **Supportive care:**
  - Oral hydration encouraged; monitor intake
  - Rest with head of bed elevated
  - Monitor temperature; acetaminophen (paracetamol) for fever and body aches
- **Antibiotic considerations:**
  - Antibiotics ONLY if bacterial superinfection suspected: purulent sputum, high procalcitonin, lobar consolidation on chest X-ray
  - If needed: azithromycin or fluoroquinolone given penicillin allergy
- **Disposition assessment:**
  - Low threshold for hospital admission given: SpO2 93% on room air, age over 65, underlying COPD, respiratory rate 22, and accessory muscle use
  - If SpO2 drops below 90%, persistent tachypnea, or worsening despite bronchodilators: recommend admission
  - If stable after observation and bronchodilators improve SpO2 to 95% or above: consider close outpatient follow-up
- **Prevention counseling:**
  - Discuss RSV vaccination (Arexvy or Abrysvo) for future prevention once recovered
  - Flu shot before next season
  - COVID booster update
- **Follow-up:** Return in 2-3 days or sooner if worsening; return precautions for severe dyspnea, SpO2 below 90%, confusion, inability to eat or drink, or high fever

## Counseling & Education Points

- Explain what RSV is and that it can cause serious illness in older adults -- it is not just a children's disease
- Discuss that RSV has no specific antiviral treatment; supportive care is key
- Emphasize importance of monitoring for worsening: increasing breathlessness, confusion, inability to eat or drink, persistent high fever
- Explain oxygen monitoring at home if pulse oximeter is available; target SpO2 above 92%
- Counsel on hand hygiene and reducing close contact with grandchildren during acute illness to prevent spread
- Discuss RSV vaccine (Arexvy or Abrysvo) for future prevention -- recommended for adults 60 and older
- Review COPD action plan for exacerbations: when to increase bronchodilators, when to start oral steroids, when to seek emergency care
- Discuss when to go to the emergency department: severe shortness of breath at rest, blue lips or fingertips, confusion, SpO2 below 90%, inability to speak in sentences

## Emotional & Behavioral Cues

- **Tone:** Concerned but trying to minimize symptoms; coughs frequently during interview; speaks slowly to conserve breath
- **Affect:** Anxious about breathing; reassured by wife's presence in waiting room; becomes visibly worried when discussing possibility of hospitalization
- **Physical:** Leans forward slightly in chair (tripod position at times); uses pursed-lip breathing intermittently; pauses mid-sentence to take deeper breaths
- **Key concern:** "I don't want to end up in the hospital. My wife would worry too much. Can I just get some medicine and go home?"
- **Volunteers:** "I thought it was just a cold from the grandkids. I didn't think it would get this bad."
- **Resistance point:** Resistant to hospitalization; may need gentle but firm explanation that oxygen levels and breathing difficulties warrant close monitoring; receptive if framed around being able to return home sooner with proper treatment

## Learning Objectives

- Recognize RSV as a significant cause of respiratory illness in older adults, particularly those with chronic lung disease
- Differentiate RSV from other causes of acute respiratory illness in the elderly (influenza, COVID-19, bacterial pneumonia, heart failure exacerbation)
- Perform appropriate diagnostic workup for acute respiratory infection in an elderly patient with COPD, including multiplex respiratory pathogen testing
- Manage acute bronchospasm and respiratory distress in the outpatient setting, including escalation of bronchodilator therapy
- Identify criteria for hospital admission in older adults with respiratory illness (oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, underlying comorbidities, functional status)
- Counsel patients on RSV prevention including vaccination for adults 60 and older
- Address patient concerns about hospitalization while ensuring safety, balancing autonomy with clinical necessity
- Apply the 4Ms framework (What Matters, Medications, Mentation, Mobility) to a geriatric respiratory illness encounter
